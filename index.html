Background

What changed, and what is still moving

Argentina in 2026 is not the Argentina of the last twenty years. The reset began in December 2023 and has held through a midterm election. But the country is mid-transition, and an honest brief has to show both the progress and the parts that have not settled.

What has been done

The government has run a primary fiscal surplus since early 2024 and stopped printing money to cover deficits. Monthly inflation, which touched 25 percent in December 2023, has been below 3.5 percent since March 2026 and printed 1.7 percent in August; the annual rate has fallen to about 34 percent and is still declining. Currency controls on individuals ended in April 2025, and the official and informal dollar rates now sit within a few percent of each other, at about 1,535 pesos to the dollar. The trade surplus exceeds USD 25 billion this year on record exports of grain, oil and gas.

Congress passed the Ley Bases in mid-2024, which created RIGI, the incentive regime for investments above USD 200 million: a thirty-year stability guarantee on taxes, customs and foreign exchange, a reduced 25 percent income-tax rate, accelerated depreciation, and free access to export dollars after a transition. A labour modernisation law followed in March 2026, with a six-month trial period, a severance cap and a union-dues cap. The government won the October 2025 midterm election and secured a US Treasury support package the same month. The country-risk spread, above 2,000 basis points when the reset began, has consolidated below 500, which is the level at which the sovereign and its large companies can borrow abroad again.

What has not settled

Growth is disappointing. After the 2025 rebound, 2026 growth is now forecast below 2 percent; industry and construction fell 5 percent in July, and consumption has not recovered. The government's approval sits around 35 percent, and the 2027 presidential election will decide whether the regime that created RIGI survives its author.

The citizenship reform is in the courts. The May 2025 emergency decree that moved naturalisation to the migration office, imposed two years of residence with no exit, and created a citizenship-by-investment route was declared null by the National Electoral Chamber on 30 June 2026. The Supreme Court appeal is pending. Separately, the Ministry of Economy cancelled the international tender to design the citizenship-by-investment programme in April 2026, before the courts ruled. So today there is no working citizenship-by-investment programme, and the path to a passport is the constitutional one: two years of legal residence and a federal judge.

RIGI is long on approvals and short on shovels. Announcements reached about USD 81 billion by June 2026, approvals USD 56 billion by August, but realised investment was under USD 1 billion at mid-year. That gap is the opportunity for anyone who can bring patient capital to approved projects, and it is also the risk.

The opportunity

Three legs: capital, residency, and a support office

Each leg stands on its own. Together they reinforce each other: the capital work creates the qualifying investments that residency requires, and the support office gives the firm and its clients a low-cost, high-quality operating base and a reason to be in the country every month.

Leg one: capital placement

The RIGI pipeline is the largest concentration of approved, incentive-backed projects in Latin America today: 24 approved projects worth USD 56 billion by August 2026, with 25 more worth about USD 111 billion under evaluation, across oil and gas (Vaca Muerta upstream, the VMOS oil pipeline, LNG), mining (copper and lithium in San Juan, Salta and Catamarca), renewables, steel and infrastructure. Applications in evaluation include YPF's USD 25 billion oil programme and Chevron's USD 13.8 billion El Trapial block.

The sponsors of these projects need equity partners, offtake-linked finance and infrastructure co-investors, and the mid-market below the USD 200 million RIGI threshold, in logistics, agri-processing, energy services and real estate, is almost entirely unbanked. Argentine mortgages are only now returning after fifteen years, and residential development in Buenos Aires is a dollar cash market, which many international investors already understand from their home markets.

Three things make the timing specific. The RIGI window closes on 8 July 2027, so sponsors who want the thirty-year stability guarantee must file before then and will sign partners early. The sovereign's return to capital markets is reopening dollar bond issuance for provinces and corporates, which creates fixed-income placements for conservative investors. And the government's privatisation and concession programme, from roads and water to hydro dams and rail, is putting operating assets in front of foreign operators for the first time in a generation.

For Gulf investors the bilateral investment treaty with the UAE, approved by Law 27.779 in October 2024, gives investor-state dispute protection, and the July 2026 business roundtable in Buenos Aires with the UAE's foreign-trade minister put energy, mining, infrastructure, agribusiness and technology on the table, with a Mercosur-wide economic partnership agreement under discussion.

Leg two: investor residency and citizenship

Two things are true at once. Argentina does not have a functioning citizenship-by-investment programme: the decree that created one is void unless the Supreme Court reinstates it, the USD 500,000 figure floated by the government in May 2025 was never regulated, and the tender to design the programme was cancelled in April 2026. And Argentina does have one of the fastest legitimate routes from investment to a passport anywhere: the investor residence category under the migration law, followed by naturalisation before a federal judge after two years of legal residence, as the Constitution has provided since 1853.

The investor category grants a renewable one-year temporary residence to a foreigner who invests in a productive, commercial or service activity approved by the Ministry of Economy, with funds brought through the banking system. The statutory minimum, 1.5 million pesos, has never been updated and is now nominal, so the test is the project's approval, not the amount. Permanent residence follows, and citizenship after two years of residence, with a passport that opens 169 destinations and Mercosur residence rights across South America. Argentina allows dual nationality, and so does Taiwan.

The product the firm can offer is therefore not "citizenship for sale". It is a structured residence-by-investment programme: an approved investment vehicle (a services company, a real estate development, a RIGI co-investment), a clean immigration file, and a two-year plan that ends in front of a judge, delivered for a per-family fee. It works under either legal scenario. If the Supreme Court restores the decree, the two-year no-exit rule applies but the path still exists; if the ruling stands, the old shortcuts for spouses and parents of Argentines return, and families with a child born in Argentina qualify almost at once.

What no one should sell is speed without residence. The 2025 investigations into paid birth-tourism schemes make the courts sensitive to files without a real life behind them. The programme has to be built on real investment and real presence, which is also what makes it durable.

Leg three: a support office at low cost and high quality

Argentina exported USD 10.1 billion of knowledge-based services in the year to March 2026, up 11.7 percent, making it the country's third-largest export sector after grain and energy: USD 6.5 billion of professional services and about USD 3 billion of software. The base is deep: strong public universities, the highest English proficiency in Latin America, and a culture of remote work for foreign clients that predates the pandemic.

The cost advantage is real but no longer extreme. A senior developer bills a median USD 40 an hour against USD 68 in the United States; mid-level engineers earn USD 35,000 to 55,000 a year. Class A office space in Buenos Aires rents for about USD 25 to 30 per square metre a month, a fraction of Dubai or Miami. The Knowledge Economy Law cuts corporate income tax by 20 to 60 percent depending on company size and turns most payroll contributions into a tax credit, with benefits running to the end of 2029. The March 2026 labour law removed the two obstacles foreign employers feared most, open-ended severance and no trial period.

The geography is the second asset. Buenos Aires is one to two hours off New York, so an Argentine team works the same day as US clients. It is seven hours behind Dubai and eleven behind Taipei, which sounds like a problem and is the opposite: an East Asian morning is Buenos Aires' evening, so an Asian client gets overnight turnaround, and a Dubai afternoon meets a Buenos Aires morning. One team in Buenos Aires, working normal hours, covers the Gulf, the Americas and East Asia without night shifts.

The quality is the third. This is not a call-centre market; it is where global banks, software companies and consultancies already run analytics, engineering and finance teams for their North American and European offices. For R.W. Coulson & Company the support office carries the firm's financial modelling, document production and AI development, including the build base for Augurnaut, the firm's AI company; for clients, the firm sets up and runs the same kind of office as a service. An Argentine SAS with ten to thirty seats, registered under the Knowledge Economy Law, does this at a third of Gulf or US cost, and it is itself a qualifying investment for the residency programme. The next chapter sets out the law it operates under.

The AI company

The legislation that governs a technology company in Argentina

A support office only earns its place if it can carry real technology work. This chapter sets out the law an AI or software business meets in Argentina, layer by layer, using Augurnaut, the firm's AI company, as the worked example: how it is formed, how it is taxed and subsidised, what it owns, what it may do with data, what AI-specific rules exist or are coming, and where the compute will sit. The short version: light-touch on AI, generous to software exporters, modern on company and labour law, and old on data.

1. The company: form, ownership, and who signs

The right vehicle is a Sociedad por Acciones Simplificada, the SAS created by the entrepreneurs' law of 2017 (Ley 27.349). It is formed digitally, needs one shareholder and one resident administrator, has no minimum capital worth mentioning, and can issue shares in classes, which matters if the company later takes Argentine investors. The Buenos Aires registry restricted the SAS between 2020 and 2023; those restrictions were reversed in 2024 and the form is again the standard start-up vehicle in the capital and in Córdoba and Mendoza. The alternative, an SRL, is the form Argentine banks know best and is a safe second choice.

A foreign parent that holds the shares, in the firm's case the Dubai entity, must first register with the companies registry under article 123 of the Companies Law (Ley 19.550), which means apostilled and translated corporate documents and a local legal representative. Budget three to six weeks for that step before the SAS itself can be filed. Directors and the legal representative bear personal liability for tax and labour debts, so the resident administrator should be a trusted professional on a proper mandate, not a nominee.

Once formed, the company registers with the tax agency (ARCA) for a CUIT, opens a bank account, enrols as an employer and, if it will hire foreign staff, registers as a sponsor with the migration office (RENURE). It then applies for the Knowledge Economy regime described below. Total set-up time with the foreign-parent step is about three months; without it, a locally held SAS can be trading in a fortnight.

Two structural points. First, the SAS is itself a qualifying investment for the investor-residence category: the shares, the capital brought in through the banking system and the payroll are exactly what the Ministry of Economy looks at. Second, the intellectual property should not sit in the Argentine company. The SAS develops under contract for the parent, which owns the code and the models; the Argentine entity holds a licence and earns a cost-plus service fee. That keeps the IP in a jurisdiction of the owner's choosing and keeps the Argentine tax base predictable.

2. Tax and incentives: the Knowledge Economy regime

Argentina's Régimen de Promoción de la Economía del Conocimiento (Ley 27.506 of 2019, amended by Ley 27.570 in 2020) is the reason to build here rather than in Uruguay or Colombia. Artificial intelligence, software development, cloud services, data science and professional services delivered abroad are all promoted activities. A registered company gets three things: a reduction of the income tax on its promoted activities of 60 percent for micro and small companies, 40 percent for medium and 20 percent for large, taking the effective rate from 35 percent to as low as 14 percent; a non-transferable tax-credit bond worth 70 percent of the employer contributions it actually pays, usable against VAT and other national taxes, and against income tax in proportion to its exports; and a certificate that stops VAT withholding on its export invoices. The benefits are guaranteed until 31 December 2029.

To qualify, at least 70 percent of revenue must come from promoted activities. Micro companies under three years old enter on that alone; everyone else must also meet two of three tests: research and development spending above a threshold of revenue, a recognised quality certification, and exports above a threshold of revenue. An AI company that sells to a Dubai parent and to US clients meets the export test on day one and the quality test with an ISO certification in year one. Registration is with the Registro Nacional de Beneficiarios; re-accreditation is every two years and requires that headcount is maintained or grown.

Exports of services are zero-rated for VAT and carry no export duty. Charges to the parent are subject to transfer-pricing rules and must be at arm's length, which is why the cost-plus service model above is the standard structure. Export proceeds are settled through the official exchange market, now a single rate; the settlement deadline is a central-bank rule that has been relaxed repeatedly since 2025 and should be checked at set-up.

3. What the company owns: software, models and data

Software is protected as a literary work under the copyright law (Ley 11.723), and the law provides that where employees are hired to write a program, the employer owns it. Contractors are different: without a written assignment, a freelancer keeps the copyright, so every contractor agreement must assign. Patents are available for technical inventions but not for software or business methods as such (Ley 24.481), so the practical protection for models, prompts, weights and pipelines is contract and the trade-secrets law (Ley 24.766), which requires that the information be kept confidential by reasonable measures.

Two AI-specific gaps are unresolved, here as in most countries: who, if anyone, is the author of AI-generated output, and whether training on copyrighted material is an infringement. Argentina has no text-and-data-mining exception, and no court has ruled. The safe course is the one a careful company takes anyway: licensed or owned training data, human authorship documented on the outputs the company sells, and terms that assign whatever rights exist to the parent.

Data protection is the oldest layer. Ley 25.326 (2000) is consent-based, requires databases to be registered with the data authority (the AAIP), gives data subjects access and deletion rights, and treats sensitive data (health, ethnicity, beliefs, sexual life) with special rules. It has one great advantage: the European Union has recognised Argentina as providing adequate protection since 2003, so personal data may flow from Europe to an Argentine team without further safeguards. Transfers out of Argentina are allowed to adequate countries and otherwise under the AAIP's model contractual clauses, which is what the firm signs with its Dubai and US entities.

Three bills to replace the law are before Congress in 2026: the executive's 2023 text reintroduced as 3397-D-2026 in July, Deputy Yeza's innovation-weighted 1751-D-2026 with a regulatory sandbox and size-based obligations, and the Carro and Doñate bills. All import the European model: accountability, privacy by design, portability, a data-protection officer, breach notification and a right to contest automated decisions. None has been voted. Building to the European standard now costs little and means the reform, when it comes, changes nothing.

4. Rules for AI itself, and the people who build it

There is no AI statute in Argentina. The data authority issued a programme on transparency and data protection in the use of AI in 2023, which is guidance, and sector regulators (banking, insurance, health) apply their own rules to automated decisions. Several bills would change that, the most complete being Deputy Giuliano's April 2026 bill, which copies the European risk classification: prohibited uses, high-risk systems with registration and human oversight, transparency duties, fines and suspension powers. The government's stance is the opposite of restrictive; it presented Stargate Argentina in October 2025 as a national project and has said it wants Argentina to be an AI hub. The firm's working assumption is no binding AI law before the 2027 election, and a European-style law within the following term. Products sold into Europe or the United States carry those jurisdictions' obligations regardless of where they are built.

Employment law is now workable for a technology employer. Ley 27.802 (March 2026) gives a six-month trial period, caps severance and bases it on a cleaner definition of salary, and caps union dues. The teleworking law (Ley 27.555) applies to remote staff: the employer provides equipment, the employee has a right to disconnect and a right to return to the office, and remote work must be agreed in writing. Software workers are usually covered by the commerce collective agreement unless the company negotiates otherwise. Argentina's strict test for disguised employment means that long-term full-time "contractors" are a liability; a support office should hire properly and use the trial period. Foreign specialists can be brought in on employer-sponsored temporary residence once the SAS is registered with the migration office.

5. Compute, energy and the Stargate signal

On 10 October 2025 the government presented Stargate Argentina: a letter of intent between OpenAI and the developer Sur Energy for a data centre in Patagonia of up to 500 MW and up to USD 25 billion at full scale, to be structured under RIGI, with OpenAI as the buyer of computing capacity rather than an investor and an unnamed global cloud developer as Sur Energy's joint-venture partner. A first 100 MW phase was talked about for 2027. As at August 2026 the Ministry of Economy had no filing on record; the letter is non-binding; and the power producer Central Puerto signed a memorandum in September 2026 to supply part of the load. It is a signal of intent, not a facility.

For a company like Augurnaut the practical position is unchanged: training and inference run on the hyperscalers' existing regions in São Paulo and the United States, and the Argentine team consumes them like any other. What Stargate and the "Súper RIGI" bill now in the Senate (a lighter regime for new activities above USD 1 billion) signal is that data-centre and energy projects in Patagonia will be the next RIGI wave, which is capital-introduction work for the desk, and that cheap Patagonian wind and gas power is the country's real asset for AI infrastructure. It also gives East Asian hardware suppliers a hook: the servers, networking and power electronics for those projects are what the TAITRA delegations sell.

Investors

Who this is for: international investors, by region

Different investors want different legs. East Asian families want optionality and a second base a hemisphere away; Gulf capital wants food, energy and infrastructure with treaty protection; US and European capital wants Vaca Muerta and copper; and international firms of every origin want a support office that costs a third of home. The desk offers each of them the leg they want and cross-sells the rest.

Taiwan and East Asia

Taiwanese capital is looking for a second base that is stable, far from Asia, dollar-based and open to dual nationality. Argentina is all four. Taiwan's nationality law lets its citizens hold a second nationality, and Argentina's does the same, so an Argentine passport is a hedge that costs a family nothing at home. The passport is not a mobility upgrade for a Taiwanese holder, who already travels visa-free to most of the world; it is a settlement right: residence and work across Mercosur, property ownership without restriction, a public university system, and a country that has taken in immigrants for 170 years.

The commercial pull is real too. TAITRA brought more than thirty Taiwanese companies to Buenos Aires on 25 August 2026 for about 200 business meetings in auto parts, bicycles and e-mobility, construction materials, electronics and IT, and the Global Taiwan Institute has singled out Argentina's lithium, copper and rare-earth opportunities as a strategic supply-chain fit. Taiwan has no embassy in Buenos Aires, but its Commercial and Cultural Office has operated there for decades and a Taiwanese community has been established in the city since the 1970s. For a Taiwanese manufacturer entering Mercosur, an Argentine subsidiary run by a resident owner is the cleanest structure, and it doubles as the family's qualifying investment.

The Gulf

The UAE imports about 90 percent of its food and has treated Argentine agriculture as a strategic supplier since the 2013 food-security agreement. The bilateral investment treaty ratified in 2024 gives Emirati investors investor-state protection, and the July 2026 Buenos Aires roundtable with Minister Al Zeyoudi and a delegation of Emirati companies covered energy, hydrocarbons, infrastructure, logistics, mining, agribusiness, technology and aviation. ADIA, Mubadala, Masdar and the Abu Dhabi Fund for Development have all sat at the table before. The firm's Dubai base sits exactly on this seam.

United States and Europe

US majors are the anchor investors in Vaca Muerta, and the US Treasury's October 2025 support package made Washington a stakeholder in the reset. European capital arrives through the Spanish and Italian heritage of half the population and through Mercosur's trade agreement with the European Union, now moving through ratification. Both bring the co-investors and offtakers that a Taiwanese or Gulf equity ticket needs beside it.

Who we do not chase

Chinese state-linked capital is already large in Argentine lithium and infrastructure and does not need an introducer; for Taiwanese clients it is also the counterparty to avoid. The firm structures through neutral jurisdictions and keeps East Asian channels separate from any mainland-linked deal.
